Interpretation of Table 10.1 and Figure 10.1: job posting volume rose from 12,782 in Farvardin 1401 to 24,017 in Mehr 1402; nearly 88% growth over 19 months. The trend is not uniform: Esfand 1401, with 5,531 postings, is the trough of the period, and Farvardin 1402, with a 229% jump relative to it, shows that this decline was mostly seasonal and temporary. The three-month moving average also confirms the long-term rise.

The Bahman and Esfand drop and the Farvardin surge are a familiar pattern of the Iranian job market: employers stop hiring before the Nowruz holidays and, immediately afterwards, the accumulated postings are published.

Interpretation of Tables 10.2 and 10.3 and Figure 10.2: the seasonal index shows a clear peak in Dey (123%) and then Mehr and Aban (115 and 112%); the lowest months are Bahman (79%), Khordad (86%) and Shahrivar (88%). In other words, the job market is more active in the second half of the year and before the end of the Solar Hijri year, and it quietens down on the eve of Nowruz and during the summer holidays.

We deliberately excluded Esfand from the seasonal index: Esfand 1401 had only 5,531 postings and Esfand 1402 is incomplete in the data; had we included it, the index would have become a misleading figure (18%) because of a single observation.

Methodological caveat: because the total market volume grew over these two years, the closing months of each year naturally have more postings. The seasonal index does not fully remove the growth trend; so read it as the “direction of the seasonal pattern”, not as a precise forecasting coefficient.

Interpretation of Tables 10.4 to 10.6 and Figure 10.3: the median salary rose from 7 million tomans in Farvardin 1401 to 13.5 million in Bahman 1402; comparing like-named months, nominal growth is 53.7%. If we assume annual inflation of 40%, real growth comes to about +13.7%, and if we assume 50%, only +3.7% remains. That is, the salaries offered in this period ran ahead of 40 percent inflation but behind 50 percent inflation.

Warning: this growth is a combination of three things: real wage growth, a shift in the mix of postings toward more expensive roles, and the rounding of medians onto discrete values (9, 11, 13.5, 17.5). The figure 53.7% should not be interpreted as wage growth for “the same job”; that would require panel data or repeated observations of the same employer.

Interpretation of Table 10.7 and Figure 10.4: the composition of demand changed over the two years. The largest declines belong to sales at the bachelor's and managerial levels (−3.1 percentage points), programming (−1.7), industrial engineering (−0.9) and DevOps (−0.6); the largest increases go to digital marketing (+1.6), salesperson and van sales representative (+1.1), finance and accounting (+0.6) and administrative clerk (+0.5).

This shift carries two messages. First, the market moved away from “senior sales” and “pure programming” roles toward operational and digital roles. Second, the growth of digital marketing is consistent with the rising share of WordPress and Google Analytics skills in Section 19; that is, this compositional change also has a skills underpinning.

Limitation: a smaller share for programming does not mean an absolute decline in demand; the number of postings in this category, 7,791 in 1402, is higher than the previous year, but its growth rate has lagged the market and its share has fallen.

10.5. In which groups and industries does the seasonal jump occur? #

The seasonal index in Section 10.2 was for the whole market. The project brief asks more explicitly: at what times and in which groups and industries do these jumps occur? If all categories rise at the same time, the seasonal peak is a “general market wave”; if the categories follow different patterns, the peak has its own date for each employer — and that difference translates directly into the hiring calendar.

Method: the same restriction as Section 10.2 (excluding Esfand, because one year of it is incomplete) and the same index definition — the number of postings in each month divided by the monthly mean of that same group × 100, so comparisons between small and large groups are valid.

# ۱۰.۵ — شاخص فصلی به تفکیک گروه
def seasonal_index_by(column, label):
    frame = (ads.loc[ads.jmonth_num.le(11)]
             .groupby([column, 'jmonth_num', 'jyear'])
             .agg(**{'تعداد': ('ad_id', 'size')})
             .reset_index())
    pivot = frame.pivot_table(index=[column, 'jmonth_num'], columns='jyear', values='تعداد', fill_value=0)
    pivot['میانگین دو سال'] = pivot.mean(axis=1)
    index = pivot['میانگین دو سال'].groupby(level=0).transform(lambda s: s / s.mean() * 100).rename('شاخص فصلی')
    wide = index.unstack('jmonth_num')
    wide.columns = [J_MONTHS[month - 1] for month in wide.columns]
    return wide


category_index = seasonal_index_by('category_group', 'دستهٔ شغلی')
industry_index = seasonal_index_by('industry_group', 'صنعت')

def peak_table(index_frame, label, min_months=11):
    table = pd.DataFrame({
        'ماه اوج': index_frame.idxmax(axis=1),
        'شاخص اوج': index_frame.max(axis=1),
        'ماه کمینه': index_frame.idxmin(axis=1),
        'شاخص کمینه': index_frame.min(axis=1),
    })
    table['دامنهٔ فصلی'] = table['شاخص اوج'] - table['شاخص کمینه']
    table = table.sort_values('شاخص اوج', ascending=False)
    return table

Interpretation of Tables 10.8 and 10.9 and Figure 10.5: the overall picture is that the market's seasonal peak is not a single general wave; 9 job categories out of 11 peak in Dey, but the intensity and the minimum months differ, and two categories follow a completely separate pattern.

  • Software development has the sharpest seasonality: a Dey index of 147.7 against 75.5 in Shahrivar — a range of 72 units. This is the pattern seen in the technology job market: budget approvals and project starts in Dey, and a hiring slump in the summer and Shahrivar.
  • Digital marketing and SEO peaks in Farvardin (132.7) and its minimum is Bahman. The reason is clear: the fiscal year for advertising and campaigns starts with Farvardin.
  • General labourer and service worker peaks in Aban (122.5), not Dey — this category is tied to the working season and seasonal projects (construction, warehousing, end-of-year sales).
  • Finance and accounting is the least volatile category (range of 42 units); because these roles are needed in every month and are not tied to a project.

At the industry level, two exceptions also appear: pharmaceuticals with a peak in Mehr (136.9) — coinciding with the reopening of schools and the cold season — and fast-moving consumer goods with a Mehr peak (117.1). The rest of the industries, including internet (135) and information technology (134.4), peak in Dey; that is, the technology industry not only has the largest volume, it also produces the largest seasonal peak.

Practical implication for the employer: the “best time to publish a posting” is not the same for everyone. A software company that posts in Shahrivar is publishing in its own lowest month of the year and must raise the salary range to compensate; whereas a digital marketing team must not miss Farvardin.
