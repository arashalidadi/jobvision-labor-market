1. Project introduction and strategic questions

6 min read · 969 words

1.1. Business problem #

In the Iranian labor market, a job posting does three things at once: it announces the employer's demand, it shows the employer's expectation level for skills and experience, and, when salary is declared, it reveals the offered price of labor. Aggregated over time, these postings build a quantitative picture of “who hires whom, with what skills, where and at what price”.

JobVision, as one of Iran's largest job platforms, has published its posting data publicly. This data offers a rare opportunity for labor market analysis, but it is raw: 43 columns of different types, free Persian text, JSON structures, missing values and one fundamental limitation — only about one third of postings declare salary.

The goal of this project is to turn this raw data into actionable insight for three audiences:

  • HR manager or business owner: With what salary and benefits should we hire? When and under what conditions should we publish the posting? Which skill really adds value?
  • Job seeker: Which skill, degree, experience and geography yields the highest financial return? How large is the gender and provincial gap?
  • Policymaker and labor market analyst: In which seasons does demand peak? Which sectors are growing or shrinking? How has geographic concentration changed?

1.2. Our role in this project #

We act as JobVision's data analysis team. The CEO and HR manager of a fictional company, “Robehrashd”, have asked us to deliver a documented picture of the market before drafting next year's hiring plan. So every analysis must be tied to one of these decisions: whom should we hire, at what price, at what time and under what conditions?

1.3. Four management questions #

The project brief raises four high-level questions. These questions are broken down into measurable indicators so that the answers can be checked and rejected or confirmed.

For example, “median salary” is computed only on postings that declare salary, whereas the “salary disclosure rate” is measured on all postings; the two cannot be read in place of each other.

1.4. Analysis approach #

The path from raw data to decision has five steps:

  1. Profiling and quality control: size, types, missingness, duplication, range and cardinality. Without this step, every later number may be meaningless.
  2. Building the analysis layer: we build a single posting-level table in which all numeric, categorical and textual features are extracted. Auxiliary tables for skills, education and benefits are also built once and reused across all sections so that computation is not repeated.
  3. Exploratory and statistical analysis: distribution, correlation, hypothesis testing and analysis of the missingness mechanism.
  4. Modeling and unsupervised learning: salary regression to identify determinants, clustering of job titles and the skills network.
  5. Translation into decisions: the compensation package table, the posting calendar and the posting text checklist.

1.5. A few basic terms #

  • Job posting: one data row. Its identifier is ad_id. One company can have several postings; therefore counting postings is not the same as counting companies.
  • Salary disclosure: Jobpost_SalaryCanBeShown == True means the posting declared the salary range. Otherwise the salary value is empty, not zero.
  • Monthly salary: the midpoint of the declared salary range, in units of million tomans per month. This number is not the contract amount; it is the price offered in the posting.
  • Hourly income: monthly salary divided by monthly working hours estimated from the “working hours” text. This indicator is needed to compare full-time, part-time and project-based jobs, because monthly salary alone does not show working hours.
  • Job category: Jobpost_MainJobCategory, the main specialization of the posting (for example “Software development and programming”). Industry: Jobpost_IndustryFa, the employer's field of activity (for example “Information technology / software and hardware”). The two are not the same: an industrial company can hire a programmer.
  • Gender preference: the text of the posting, not the gender of the person hired. “Men only” means the employer made hiring a man a requirement.
  • Age range: the minimum and maximum age requested by the employer, not the actual age of job seekers.
  • Outlier: a value far from the body of the distribution. We do not remove outliers; we flag them, keep them in the description and restrict them only in modeling, using a transparent rule.

1.6. From number to insight #

In this notebook every claim has three components: number, comparison and limitation. For example, “the median salary of the information technology category is 14.5 million tomans and 38% above the median of the whole market” is an insight; the number 14.5 on its own is not information. At the end of each section, the “Answer” and “Business summary” sentences follow the same structure.

Golden rule: we never report an indicator without its denominator and sample. Wherever the sample is small we give a warning; wherever the difference is not significant we say so.
