20. Profiling and clustering of employers [bonus]

6 min read · 5 tables · 1 figures · 4 code cells

Since company names are anonymized, we describe the employer by observable features: size, age, ownership, scope of activity, industry, province, number of benefits, and posting behavior (salary disclosure, remote work). The data is mixed; therefore we use the Gower distance, which combines both numeric and categorical variables into a single distance measure, and then hierarchical clustering is performed on the same distance matrix.

from gower import gower_matrix
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

employer_numeric = ['company_size_rank', 'company_age_rank', 'benefit_count', 'skill_count',
                    'exp_required', 'remote_flag', 'transparency_flag']
employer_categorical = ['ownership', 'activity_type', 'company_industry_main', 'Jobpost_ProvinceFa',
                        'Jobpost_MainJobCategory']
employer_source = ads.assign(exp_required=ads.Jobpost_RequiredExperienceYears,
                             remote_flag=ads.Jobpost_IsRemote.astype(int),
                             transparency_flag=ads.salary_disclosed.astype(int))
employer_source = employer_source.dropna(subset=employer_numeric + employer_categorical)
employer_features = employer_source[employer_numeric + employer_categorical].copy()
for column in employer_categorical:
    employer_features[column] = employer_features[column].astype(str).astype('category').cat.codes
employer_features = employer_features.astype(float)

sample_index = employer_features.sample(min(4000, len(employer_features)),
                                        random_state=RANDOM_STATE).index
gower_matrix_employer = gower_matrix(employer_features.loc[sample_index].values,
                                     cat_features=[False] * len(employer_numeric) +
                                                  [True] * len(employer_categorical))

selection_rows = []
for k in [3, 4, 5, 6]:
    labels = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=k, metric='precomputed',
                                     linkage='average').fit_predict(gower_matrix_employer)
    selection_rows.append({'k': k, 'سیلوئت گاور': silhouette_score(gower_matrix_employer, labels,
                                                                   metric='precomputed'),
                           'کوچک‌ترین خوشه': int(pd.Series(labels).value_counts().min())})
selection = pd.DataFrame(selection_rows).set_index('k')
show_table('20.1', 'انتخاب تعداد تیپ کارفرما روی ماتریس فاصله گاور', selection,
           formats={'سیلوئت گاور': 'ratio', 'کوچک‌ترین خوشه': 'int'},
           sample=f'{len(sample_index):,} آگهی نمونه', period=False, index_name='k',
           note='سیلوئت بالاتر و خوشه‌های متعادل‌تر بهترند؛ ساختار نهایی باید قابل تفسیر هم باشد.')

best_employer_k = 4
labels_employer = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=best_employer_k, metric='precomputed',
                                          linkage='average').fit_predict(gower_matrix_employer)
employer_sample = employer_source.loc[sample_index].assign(تیپ=labels_employer)

typology_rows = []
for cluster in range(best_employer_k):
    subset = employer_sample.loc[employer_sample['تیپ'].eq(cluster)]
    typology_rows.append({
        'تیپ': f'تیپ {cluster + 1}', 'تعداد آگهی نمونه': len(subset),
        'سهم نمونه': len(subset) / len(employer_sample) * 100,
        'میانه اندازه شرکت': subset.company_size_rank.median(),
        'میانه مزایا': subset.benefit_count.median(),
        'نرخ افشای حقوق': subset.salary_disclosed.mean() * 100,
        'نرخ دورکاری': subset.Jobpost_IsRemote.mean() * 100,
        'صنعت غالب': subset.company_industry_main.value_counts().idxmax(),
        'استان غالب': subset.Jobpost_ProvinceFa.value_counts().idxmax(),
    })
employer_typology = pd.DataFrame(typology_rows).set_index('تیپ').sort_values('تعداد آگهی نمونه',
                                                                             ascending=False)
show_table('20.2', 'تیپ‌شناسی کارفرمایان بر پایه ویژگی‌های شرکت و رفتار آگهی', employer_typology,
           formats={'تعداد آگهی نمونه': 'int', 'سهم نمونه': 'pct', 'میانه اندازه شرکت': 'hours',
                    'میانه مزایا': 'hours', 'نرخ افشای حقوق': 'pct',
                    'نرخ دورکاری': 'pct'},
           sample=f'{len(employer_sample):,} آگهی نمونه', period=False, index_name='تیپ کارفرما',
           note='مقیاس اندازه شرکت ۱ تا ۷ است؛ عدد بزرگ‌تر یعنی شرکت بزرگ‌تر. ستون‌های دسته‌ای، غالب هر تیپ را نشان می‌دهند.')

Interpretation of Tables 20.1 to 20.3 and Figures 20.1 and 20.2: The Gower distance over a sample of 4,000 job postings shows a clear structure: two large types and two small, distinct types.

  • Type 1 (75.6% of the sample): Large and medium employers with 3 benefits, salary disclosure of 32%, and remote work of 5.5% — the “market core”.
  • Type 3 (24.1%): Small employers with 1 benefit, disclosure of 39%, and remote work of 10.4% — the “flexible small employer”.
  • Type 2 (8 postings): Large employers with 11 benefits and disclosure of 87.5% — the “transparent and generous employer”; a very small but distinct minority.
  • Type 4 (2 postings): A small employer in Alborz with 0.5 benefit — practically an outlier.

This typology shows that “salary transparency” and “generosity in benefits” are a rare but existing pattern in Iran; if your company is in Type 1, by disclosing salaries and adding two benefits you can quickly stand out from the mass of the market.

Limitation: The last two types have only 10 postings and should not be generalized as a “market segment”; they are marginal patterns that the algorithm has separated out. A silhouette of 0.23 shows that the boundaries are moderate and not entirely clean.

20.5. The structure of competition: where is competition intense and where does a posting stand out? #

The third question of the project’s outlook was: “What is the structure of competition among employers, and what can a business do to differentiate its job postings?”. The data anonymizes the employer’s name, so we measure competition at the market level, not the company level. Four indicators suffice:

  • Posting volume and share: the more, the greater the competition for attraction.
  • Annual growth: a market that grows quickly has more future competition.
  • Coefficient of variation of salary (standard deviation ÷ mean): the higher it is, the less standardized the pricing, and the more room there is for bargaining and differentiation.
  • Disclosure rate: a low-transparency market is a field for postings that stand out through transparency.

Key numbers: 20.3

  • Market concentration (HHI of category shares): 1852 out of 10,000
  • Highest-volume category: other categories — 36.9%
  • Highest salary dispersion: Sales and marketing - bachelor's and managerial levels — 58.9%
  • Least transparent high-volume category: network / DevOps / hardware and software support — disclosure 18.2%

Interpretation of Table 20.5 and Key Numbers 20.3: The competitive structure of Iran’s labor market is not concentrated but is unbalanced; the concentration index (HHI) is 1852 out of 10,000, which is a long way from the threshold of 2500 (a concentrated market). That is, no single category holds the field of competition by itself, and competition is spread across all fronts.

  • Where is competition just now flaring up? Digital marketing with growth of +153.6%, general laborer (+78.3%), salesperson and van sales rep (+76.3%), and office clerk (+70.5%) are the fastest-growing arms. Against them, software development has grown only +6.4% — that is, this very market, which has the greatest visibility, is in fact the most mature and most saturated segment, and competition for attracting staff in it is less dynamic.
  • Where is there more bargaining? The coefficient of variation of salary is highest in professional sales and marketing at 58.9%, then software (52.8%) and finance (50.2%); that is, the market does not agree on the price of these roles, and a posting that states the salary transparently gains the most from differentiation in precisely these categories. By contrast, general laborer (28.6%) and office clerk (32.9%) are the least dispersed — the price of these roles is practically standardized and close to the minimum wage.
  • Where is transparency low? Network/DevOps (18%), industrial engineering (23%), and software (25%) are the least transparent high-volume categories, while general laborer (47.8%) and salesperson (44.6%) are the most transparent. The pattern is clear: the more knowledge-intensive the role, the more likely salaries are hidden — and this is the biggest opportunity for differentiation.
  • Remote work is elsewhere: Digital marketing (24.5%) and software (21.5%) have the highest remote-work rates; finance and accounting are practically zero (0.6%).

An operational answer to the question “how do we differentiate our posting?” — four rules documented from this very data:

  1. In low-transparency categories, disclose the salary. In network/DevOps, where only 18% of competitors include a salary, stating a range immediately sets you apart from 82% of the market and — based on Section 9 — transparency in the market for mid-level roles is the main channel of attraction.
  2. For high-variance roles, give a range, not a number. In professional sales with a coefficient of variation of 59%, a fixed number drives away half the applicants; a range close to the median of that category is both transparent and realistic.
  3. For standard roles, compete on speed and conditions, not salary. For general laborers and office clerks, the price is nearly fixed; non-cash benefits and the timing of publication (Section 10.5) are the real levers.
  4. Take timing seriously. Publishing in Dey for technology and finance, in Farvardin for digital marketing, and in Aban for service roles increases the chance of being seen; publishing in Bahman — the lowest month for all categories — lowers that chance.
