Reading Figures 8.1 and 8.2 and Table 8.1: The strongest relationship is the negative one between weekly hours and hourly income (−0.68), which is partly mechanical: hourly income is constructed by dividing salary by hours. More important is the gap between Pearson and Spearman in the salary–hourly income pair (0.44 versus 0.69); this gap shows that the relationship between the two variables is non-linear and that their ranks move together more than their linear values do.

Work experience correlates 0.40 with salary; no single variable explains more than 0.5 of the variation in salary. This is a finding in itself: salary is not a single-factor variable but a combination of occupation, skills, geography, company size and experience. That is why the multivariate model in Section 16 is necessary.

Reading Table 8.2: Variance inflation factors are below 1.5 for every numeric variable; that is, there is no serious multicollinearity. This result is somewhat counter-intuitive, because one would normally expect experience and age, or company size and number of benefits, to overlap; but in this data the correlations are mild and each variable carries relatively independent information. The ridge model coefficients in Section 16 are therefore interpretable. Limitation: this test was run only on numeric variables and does not cover categorical variables.

from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor

vif_cols = ['salary_mid', 'Jobpost_RequiredExperienceYears', 'age_mid', 'skill_count', 'benefit_count',
            'company_size_rank', 'company_age_rank', 'weekly_hours']
vif_source = hourly_ready[vif_cols].dropna()
vif_matrix = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(vif_source)), vif_source.values])
vif_values = [variance_inflation_factor(vif_matrix, position) for position in range(1, vif_matrix.shape[1])]
vif_table = pd.DataFrame({'عامل تورم واریانس': vif_values},
                         index=[corr_map[name] for name in vif_cols]).sort_values('عامل تورم واریانس', ascending=False)
vif_table['وضعیت'] = np.select([vif_table['عامل تورم واریانس'].ge(10), vif_table['عامل تورم واریانس'].ge(5)],
                               ['هم‌خطی شدید', 'هم‌خطی متوسط'], default='قابل قبول')
show_table('8.2', 'عامل تورم واریانس متغیرهای عددی', vif_table,
           formats={'عامل تورم واریانس': 'score'}, sample=f'{len(vif_source):,} آگهی کامل',
           period=False, index_name='متغیر',
           note='VIF بیش از ۵ نشانه هم‌خطی متوسط و بیش از ۱۰ نشانه هم‌خطی شدید است.')

Reading Figures 8.3 and 8.4 and Table 8.3: The experience curve shows the “diminishing returns” pattern clearly. The median salary rises from 9 million in postings with no experience requirement to 11 million at 1 to 2 years, 13.5 million at 3 years, 17.5 million at 4 to 6 years and 22.5 million at 7 years and above. The main jumps happen at years 1, 3 and 4, and between 4 and 6 years the curve is almost flat; that is, after five years each additional year of experience on its own does not create meaningful added value, and value depends more on the type of role and skills.

The age curve is also rising but with a gentle slope: the median goes from 9 million in the 22 to 28 years bracket to 11 million at 28 to 34 years and 13.5 million at 34 to 40 years. In the brackets above 40 years the median reaches 17.5 and 22.5 million, but the sample in these brackets is small and this should not be read as a definitive “income peak”; a more detailed analysis with an industry breakdown comes in Section 13.

An important point for management: if a posting asks for “3 years of experience”, the pay difference from “no experience” is about 50%; but if you want the same role with 5 years of experience, you should pay roughly the same salary level as for 3 years. That means requiring more than 3 years of experience does not create any additional pay value in most occupations.
