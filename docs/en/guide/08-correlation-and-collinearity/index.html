Interpretation of figures 8.1 and 8.2 and table 8.1: The strongest relationship is the negative relationship between weekly hours and hourly income (−0.68), which is partly mechanical: hourly income is constructed by dividing salary by hours. More important is the difference between Pearson and Spearman for the “salary–hourly income” pair (0.44 versus 0.69); this difference shows that the relationship between these two variables is non-linear and that the ranks are more aligned than the linear values.

Work experience correlates with salary at 0.40; no variable alone explains more than 0.5 of the variation in salary. This is itself a finding: salary is not a single-factor variable but a combination of job, skill, geography, company size, and experience. That is why the multivariate model in section 16 is necessary.

Interpretation of table 8.2: The variance inflation factor for all numeric variables is below 1.5, meaning there is no serious multicollinearity. This result is somewhat counterintuitive, since experience and age, or company size and the number of benefits, would usually be expected to overlap; but in this data the correlations are mild and each variable carries relatively independent information. Therefore the coefficients of the ridge model in section 16 are interpretable. Limitation: this test was performed only on numeric variables and does not cover categorical variables.

Interpretation of figures 8.3 and 8.4 and table 8.3: The experience curve clearly shows the “diminishing returns” pattern. The median salary rises from 9 million in postings with no experience to 11 million for 1 to 2 years, 13.5 million for 3 years, 17.5 million for 4 to 6 years, and 22.5 million for 7 years and above. The main jumps occur at years 1, 3, and 4, and between 4 and 6 years it flattens out almost completely; that is, after five years, each additional year of experience alone does not create significant added value, and the greater value depends on the type of role and skill.

The age curve is also upward but with a gentle slope: the median rises from 9 million in the 22 to 28 age range to 11 million in 28 to 34 and 13.5 million in 34 to 40. In ranges above 40, the median reaches 17.5 and 22.5 million, but the sample in these ranges is small and should not be read as a definitive “income peak”; a more precise analysis comes in section 13 with a breakdown by industry.

An important point for management: if a posting asks for “3 years of experience”, the wage difference from “no experience” is about 50%; but if you want the same role with 5 years of experience, you should pay approximately the same salary level as 3 years. This means requiring more than 3 years of experience does not create additional pay value in most jobs.

8.4. Scatter and comparison figures: the relationship at the posting level #

The correlation matrix tells the direction and strength of the relationship, but it hides three things: the shape of the relationship, the dispersion within each level, and the points that break the pattern. This subsection shows these with three views:

  1. Weekly hours × hourly income — the mechanics of the hourly index: the more hours, the larger the denominator and the lower the hourly income.
  2. Experience × monthly salary with a median line and an interquartile range band — an upward relationship with diminishing returns in a single view.
  3. Number of benefits × salary as a box plot — comparing distributions, not just the median.

For readability, in the scatter plots a sample of 9,000 postings (from hourly_ready) is plotted with low opacity, and the median line is computed on the entire original data.

Interpretation of tables 8.4 and 8.5 and figure 8.5: The three views show three different things.

a) Working hours do not raise the monthly salary; they lower the hourly income. The median hourly income falls from 115 thousand tomans in postings with fewer than 40 hours to 66 in the 45 to 48 hour range and 47 in postings over 52 hours — a 59 percent drop. More important is that the median monthly salary does not rise along this path: 13.5 million for 40 to 48 hours, but 11 million for 49 hours and more. That is, postings that demand long hours are not only cheaper per hour but also pay less per month; these postings are mostly in service and production categories.

b) Experience has an upward relationship with diminishing returns. The median line rises from 9 million (no experience) to 22.5 million (7 years and above), but the slope is steep in the early years and becomes gentle after 5 years. The width of the quartile band also increases with experience: at zero experience it is narrow (the market has roughly one price) and at high experience it is wide (diversity of roles and industries). This widening explains why linear models have greater error at high experience.

c) “Number of benefits” is a step-like signal, not a continuous one. The median salary stays at 11 million for zero to three benefits and jumps to 13.5 million from four benefits and above (+22.7%). Hourly income also rises from 58 to 67 thousand tomans. The boxes also show that dispersion is greater in the high-benefit groups, meaning “many benefits” is more a sign of a larger company than itself a cause of higher salary.

A side observation useful to managers: postings with no benefits have the highest remote work rate (14.7% versus 6.5% in the four-benefit group). That is, part of the market substitutes “flexibility” for “welfare benefits” — the same part that is proposed in section 22 as a non-cash compensation package.

8.5. Premium of benefits and remote work with controls #

The simple comparison of medians (table 8.5) showed that postings with benefits have higher salaries, but it was not clear whether this effect comes from the benefit itself or from a larger company and a heavier role. Here, using a ridge model, experience, working hours, company size and age, degree level, and number of skills are simultaneously controlled to obtain the net premium of each benefit and the premium of remote work.

# ۸.۵ — پاداش مزایا و دورکاری با کنترل
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

premium_frame = paid.copy()
premium_frame['is_remote'] = premium_frame.Jobpost_IsRemote.astype(int)
premium_frame['exp_years'] = premium_frame.Jobpost_RequiredExperienceYears.fillna(0)
premium_frame['hours'] = premium_frame.weekly_hours.fillna(premium_frame.weekly_hours.median())
premium_frame['size_rank'] = premium_frame.company_size_rank.fillna(premium_frame.company_size_rank.median())
premium_frame['age_rank'] = premium_frame.company_age_rank.fillna(premium_frame.company_age_rank.median())
premium_frame['degree_rank'] = premium_frame.max_degree_rank.fillna(0)

ext_top_benefits = benefits_long.groupby('benefit').ad_id.nunique().sort_values(ascending=False).head(12).index
benefit_dummies = (benefits_long.loc[benefits_long.benefit.isin(ext_top_benefits)]
                   .assign(flag=1)
                   .pivot_table(index='ad_id', columns='benefit', values='flag', fill_value=0)
                   .reindex(premium_frame.ad_id, fill_value=0)
                   .add_prefix('مزیت: '))
premium_frame = pd.concat([premium_frame.reset_index(drop=True), benefit_dummies.reset_index(drop=True)], axis=1)

premium_numeric = ['exp_years', 'hours', 'size_rank', 'age_rank', 'degree_rank', 'skill_count', 'is_remote']
premium_features = premium_numeric + list(benefit_dummies.columns)
premium_model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), Ridge(alpha=1.0))
premium_model.fit(premium_frame[premium_features], premium_frame.log_salary)
premium_coef = pd.Series(premium_model.named_steps['ridge'].coef_, index=premium_features)

benefit_value_table = pd.DataFrame({
    'تعداد آگهی': [int(benefit_dummies[column].sum()) for column in benefit_dummies.columns],
    'پاداش خالص (٪)': [(math.exp(premium_coef[column]) - 1) * 100 for column in benefit_dummies.columns],
}, index=[column.replace('مزیت: ', '') for column in benefit_dummies.columns])
benefit_value_table = benefit_value_table.sort_values('پاداش خالص (٪)', ascending=False)
show_table('8.6', 'پاداش خالص هر مزیت بر حقوق (با کنترل سابقه، ساعت، اندازه و مقطع)', benefit_value_table,
           formats={'تعداد آگهی': 'int', 'پاداش خالص (٪)': 'pp'},
           sample=f'{len(premium_frame):,} آگهی با حقوق اعلام‌شده', index_name='مزیت',
           note='پاداش خالص = اثر مزیت پس از کنترل هم‌زمان سایر ویژگی‌ها؛ میانگین دو سال.')

remote_premium_rows = []
for label, subset in [('کل بازار', premium_frame)] + [
        (category, premium_frame.loc[premium_frame.Jobpost_MainJobCategory.eq(category)])
        for category in ads.Jobpost_MainJobCategory.value_counts().head(6).index]:
    remote = subset.loc[subset.is_remote.eq(1), 'salary_mid']
    onsite = subset.loc[subset.is_remote.eq(0), 'salary_mid']
    median_gap = (remote.median() / onsite.median() - 1) * 100 if len(remote) and len(onsite) else np.nan
    remote_premium_rows.append({'گروه': label, 'تعداد دورکاری': int(subset.is_remote.sum()),
                                'میانه دورکاری': remote.median() if len(remote) else np.nan,
                                'میانه حضوری': onsite.median() if len(onsite) else np.nan,
                                'اختلاف میانه (٪)': median_gap,

Interpretation of tables 8.6 and 8.7 and the key premium numbers: After controlling for experience, hours, company size and age, and degree level:

  • Commission is the strongest benefit at +5.5%, followed by loan at +2.8% and flexible working hours at +2.3%. This is logical: commission means part of the income is tied to performance, and the higher-risk sales postings set a higher base.
  • Supplementary health insurance has an effect of only +1.3% and bonus +0.3%; that is, these have become a “baseline expectation” in the Iranian market, not a differentiating benefit. For an employer who wants their posting to stand out, emphasizing these has no effect.
  • Transportation service is the only benefit with a negative effect (−0.9%): this benefit is indicative of blue-collar and shift roles, so it is seen more in low-salary postings.
  • Remote work: there is no universal rule. The net effect of remote work in the model is +3.2%, but table 8.7 shows that this average hides two contradictory realities: in the market as a whole, the median salary for remote and on-site work is equal (11 million), but the hourly income of remote work is 41% higher (because it requires fewer hours); whereas in sales, remote work pays 33% less. So “remote work is a premium” is true only for technical and administrative roles, not for sales.
  • The net effect of experience in this model is +21.6% per year and the net effect of hours is −0.7% per hour — the same two opposing directions we saw in sections 8.4 and 16.4.
