9. Salary missingness: random or dependent?

4 min read · 7 tables · 3 code cells

Three-quarters of postings do not state a salary. The key question is whether this concealment is related to observable characteristics (company size, province, job category, type of employment) or not. If the missingness is dependent and structured, there are two important consequences: first, salary analysis becomes biased toward a particular segment of the market; second, “disclose or not disclose” itself becomes a studyable employer behaviour.

Reading Tables 9.1 to 9.4: Salary missingness is thoroughly structured. The disclosure rate is inversely related to company size: companies with 11 to 50 employees publish a salary for 43.4% of their postings, while companies with 1001 to 5000 employees do so for only 16.6%. This 26.8 percentage-point difference is the largest gap across all dimensions: a small company competing for talent in an unknown market uses transparency as a competitive tool, whereas a large company leans on its brand and career path.

Job category shows a clear pattern too: postings for general labourers (47.8%), salespeople and field reps (44.6%) and digital marketing (42.3%) are the most transparent, while DevOps and technical support (18.2%) and programming (24.9%) are the least transparent. In other words, the more knowledge-intensive the role and the higher the pay, the less public disclosure.

Remote work makes the largest difference of any binary feature: 52.5% of remote postings state a salary, against 31.6% of on-site postings. A remote posting competes with the global market, which makes salary transparency more necessary for it. Provinces do not show a large difference (30.7% in Alborz to 39.5% in East Azerbaijan); so “geography” alone is not explanatory.

9.2. Significance testing: chi-square and Cramér's effect size #

Python · 24 lines
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

dimension_map = {'اندازه شرکت': 'Company_SizeFa', 'استان (تهران/سایر)': 'province_group',
                 'دسته شغلی': 'category_group', 'صنعت': 'industry_group',
                 'نوع همکاری': 'Jobpost_WorkTypeFa', 'رده سازمانی': 'seniority',
                 'دورکاری': 'Jobpost_IsRemote', 'کارآموزی': 'Jobpost_IsInternship',
                 'الزام پایان خدمت': 'Jobpost_RequiredMilitaryServiceCard', 'مالکیت': 'ownership',
                 'دامنه فعالیت': 'activity_type'}
chi_rows = []
for label, column in dimension_map.items():
    table = pd.crosstab(ads[column], ads.salary_disclosed)
    chi2, p_value, dof, _ = chi2_contingency(table)
    total = table.values.sum()
    cramers_v = math.sqrt(chi2 / (total * min(table.shape[0] - 1, table.shape[1] - 1)))
    chi_rows.append({'بُعد': label, 'تعداد سطوح': table.shape[0], 'آماره خی‌دو': chi2,
                     'درجه آزادی': dof, 'p-value': p_value, 'کرامر V': cramers_v})
chi_table = pd.DataFrame(chi_rows).set_index('بُعد').sort_values('کرامر V', ascending=False)
chi_table['قدرت رابطه'] = np.select([chi_table['کرامر V'].ge(0.3), chi_table['کرامر V'].ge(0.1)],
                                    ['قوی', 'متوسط'], default='ضعیف')
show_table('9.5', 'آزمون استقلال خی‌دو بین افشای حقوق و ویژگی‌های آگهی', chi_table,
           formats={'تعداد سطوح': 'int', 'آماره خی‌دو': 'int', 'درجه آزادی': 'int',
                    'p-value': 'pvalue', 'کرامر V': 'ratio'},
           sample=f'{len(ads):,} آگهی', period=False, index_name='بُعد',
           note='کرامر V زیر ۰٫۱ رابطه ضعیف، ۰٫۱ تا ۰٫۳ متوسط و بالای ۰٫۳ قوی تلقی می‌شود.')

Reading Table 9.5: All chi-square tests are significant at the 0.001 level; that is, the hypothesis of completely random missingness (MCAR) is rejected. But statistical significance is not the same as effect size: the largest Cramér's V belongs to company size (0.188) and job category (0.147), which falls in the “weak to moderate” range; remote work (0.112) and internship (0.094) follow, and the remaining dimensions are below 0.1. So the dependence is real, but none of these features determines the disclosure rate on its own.

A methodological note: with a sample of 360 thousand, even small differences become significant; that is why reporting effect size alongside the p-value is essential.

    'سابقه لازم': ads.Jobpost_RequiredExperienceYears,

    'دورکاری': ads.Jobpost_IsRemote.astype(int),
    'کارآموزی': ads.Jobpost_IsInternship.astype(int),
    'اولویت نیروی بومی': ads.Jobpost_PriorityWithLocalCandidate.astype(int),
    'الزام پایان خدمت': ads.Jobpost_RequiredMilitaryServiceCard.astype(int),
    'دارای رشته تحصیلی': ads.has_academic.astype(int),
    'تهران': ads.province_group.eq('تهران').astype(int),
})
categorical_block = pd.get_dummies(ads[['Jobpost_WorkTypeFa', 'seniority', 'ownership', 'activity_type']],
                                   columns=['Jobpost_WorkTypeFa', 'seniority', 'ownership', 'activity_type'],
                                   drop_first=True, dtype=float)
logit_design = sm.add_constant(pd.concat([numeric_block, categorical_block], axis=1), has_constant='add')
logit_target = ads.salary_disclosed.astype(float)
logit_model = sm.Logit(logit_target, logit_design).fit(disp=0, maxiter=200)

coefficients = logit_model.params.drop('const')
p_values = logit_model.pvalues.drop('const')
odds = np.exp(coefficients)
strength = coefficients.abs().sort_values(ascending=False)
top_coefficients = pd.DataFrame({
    'ضریب': coefficients.loc[strength.index],
    'نسبت شانس': odds.loc[strength.index],
    'p-value': p_values.loc[strength.index],
}).head(15)
top_coefficients['اثر بر احتمال افشا'] = np.where(top_coefficients['ضریب'] > 0, 'افزایشی', 'کاهشی')
show_table('9.6', 'پانزده متغیر اثرگذار بر احتمال افشای حقوق', top_coefficients,
           formats={'ضریب': 'score', 'نسبت شانس': 'score', 'p-value': 'pvalue'},
           sample=f'{len(ads):,} آگهی', period=False, index_name='متغیر',
           note='نسبت شانس بزرگ‌تر از ۱ یعنی متغیر احتمال افشا را بالا می‌برد و کمتر از ۱ یعنی پایین می‌آورد.')

Key figures: salary disclosure

  • Overall disclosure rate: 33.1%
  • Highest rate by company size: 11 to 50 employees — 43.4%
  • Lowest rate by company size: 1001 to 5000 employees — 16.6%
  • Strongest dimension (Cramér V): company size — 0.188

Reading Tables 9.6 and 9.7 and the final answer: The logistic regression model shows that the odds of salary disclosure are 2.76 times the baseline for general labourer postings, 2.36 times for remote postings and 2.33 times for salesperson/field rep postings; conversely, part-time postings have lower odds (0.65) of disclosure. All these coefficients are statistically significant, but the model's performance is limited: McFadden's pseudo-R2 is 0.05, the area under the ROC curve is 0.65, and classification accuracy (68.1%) is only 1.2 percentage points better than the baseline model (always “not disclosed”).

Answer to the project question: Salary missingness is not completely random (MCAR); it depends on observable variables — especially company size, job category and remote work. The missingness mechanism is therefore of the MAR type (dependent on observed data). But the explained share is small; that is, a large part of the disclosure decision is each employer's internal policy and unobservable characteristics.

Practical implication: The salary means and medians in this data are biased toward small companies, administrative/sales/service roles, and remote and contract postings. For specialized roles and large companies, these numbers show the floor of the market, not the actual level of competition. Section 22 accounts for this limitation in the salary recommendations.
