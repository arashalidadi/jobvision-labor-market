9. The mechanism of salary missingness: random or dependent?

7 min read · 10 tables · 1 figures · 6 code cells

Three-quarters of postings do not disclose salary. The key question is whether this concealment is related to observable characteristics (company size, province, job category, type of cooperation) or not. If the missingness is dependent and structured, it has two important consequences: first, the salary analysis is biased toward a particular segment of the market; second, “disclosure or non-disclosure” itself becomes a studyable employer behavior.

Interpretation of tables 9.1 to 9.4: Salary missingness is highly structured. The disclosure rate is inversely related to company size: companies with 11 to 50 employees publish 43.4% of their postings with salary, while companies with 1001 to 5000 employees publish only 16.6%. This difference of 26.8 percentage points is the largest gap across all dimensions: a small company uses transparency as a competitive tool to attract people in an unknown market, while a large company relies on brand and career path.

Job category also has a clear pattern: postings for general laborers (47.8%), salespeople and medical reps (44.6%), and digital marketing (42.3%) are the most transparent, while DevOps and technical support (18.2%) and programming (24.9%) have the least transparency. That is, the more knowledge-based the role and the higher the salary, the less public disclosure.

Remote work makes the biggest difference for a binary characteristic: 52.5% of remote work postings disclose salary, versus 31.6% of on-site postings. A remote work posting competes with the global market, and salary transparency becomes more essential for it. Provinces do not show a big difference (30.7% in Alborz to 39.5% in East Azerbaijan); therefore “geography” alone is not explanatory.

9.2. Significance test: chi-square and Cramér's effect size #

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

dimension_map = {'اندازه شرکت': 'Company_SizeFa', 'استان (تهران/سایر)': 'province_group',
                 'دسته شغلی': 'category_group', 'صنعت': 'industry_group',
                 'نوع همکاری': 'Jobpost_WorkTypeFa', 'رده سازمانی': 'seniority',
                 'دورکاری': 'Jobpost_IsRemote', 'کارآموزی': 'Jobpost_IsInternship',
                 'الزام پایان خدمت': 'Jobpost_RequiredMilitaryServiceCard', 'مالکیت': 'ownership',
                 'دامنه فعالیت': 'activity_type'}
chi_rows = []
for label, column in dimension_map.items():
    table = pd.crosstab(ads[column], ads.salary_disclosed)
    chi2, p_value, dof, _ = chi2_contingency(table)
    total = table.values.sum()
    cramers_v = math.sqrt(chi2 / (total * min(table.shape[0] - 1, table.shape[1] - 1)))
    chi_rows.append({'بُعد': label, 'تعداد سطوح': table.shape[0], 'آماره خی‌دو': chi2,
                     'درجه آزادی': dof, 'p-value': p_value, 'کرامر V': cramers_v})
chi_table = pd.DataFrame(chi_rows).set_index('بُعد').sort_values('کرامر V', ascending=False)
chi_table['قدرت رابطه'] = np.select([chi_table['کرامر V'].ge(0.3), chi_table['کرامر V'].ge(0.1)],
                                    ['قوی', 'متوسط'], default='ضعیف')
show_table('9.5', 'آزمون استقلال خی‌دو بین افشای حقوق و ویژگی‌های آگهی', chi_table,
           formats={'تعداد سطوح': 'int', 'آماره خی‌دو': 'int', 'درجه آزادی': 'int',
                    'p-value': 'pvalue', 'کرامر V': 'ratio'},
           sample=f'{len(ads):,} آگهی', period=False, index_name='بُعد',
           note='کرامر V زیر ۰٫۱ رابطه ضعیف، ۰٫۱ تا ۰٫۳ متوسط و بالای ۰٫۳ قوی تلقی می‌شود.')

Interpretation of table 9.5: All chi-square tests are significant at the 0.001 level, meaning the assumption of “missing completely at random (MCAR)” is rejected. But statistical significance is not the same as effect size: the largest Cramér's V belongs to company size (0.188) and job category (0.147), which falls in the “weak to moderate” range; remote work (0.112) and internship (0.094) follow, and the other dimensions are below 0.1. That is, the dependency is real, but none of these characteristics alone determines the disclosure rate.

A methodological note: with a sample of 360 thousand, even small differences become significant; that is why reporting effect size alongside the p-value is essential.

Interpretation of tables 9.6 and 9.7 and the final answer: The logistic regression model shows that the odds of salary disclosure for general laborer postings is 2.76 times the baseline level, for remote work postings 2.36 times, and for salesperson/medical rep 2.33 times; in contrast, part-time postings have lower odds (0.65) of disclosure. All these coefficients are statistically significant, but the model's performance is limited: McFadden's pseudo-R2 is 0.05 and the area under the ROC curve is 0.65, and the classification accuracy (68.1%) is only 1.2 percentage points better than the baseline model (always “not disclosed”).

Answer to the project question: Salary missingness is not missing completely at random (MCAR); it depends on observable variables — especially company size, job category, and remote work. Therefore the missingness model is of the MAR type (dependent on the observed data). But the explained share is small; that is, a large part of the disclosure decision is each employer's internal policy and unobservable characteristics.

Practical implication: The salary means and medians in this data are biased toward small companies, administrative/sales/service roles, and remote and contract postings. For specialized roles and large companies, these numbers indicate the market floor, not the actual level of competition. In section 22 this limitation is taken into account in the salary recommendations.

9.4. Frequency of disclosed salary values and small-sample groups #

The project brief has two explicit requirements in this very section: first, the frequency of available salary values should be analyzed — because all wage analyses stand on these numbers, and their shape determines which statistic is valid; second, any group in which the number of postings with disclosed salary is close to zero should be reported, so the reader knows which result is built on a small sample and should not be generalized to the whole market.

In this subsection we first see what numbers employers actually write (not just the median and quartiles), and then we list the small-sample groups across four key dimensions.

Interpretation of table 9.8 and figure 9.1: The most important finding of this subsection is the extreme discreteness of salaries: 119,048 postings with salary have only 40 unique values. The five most frequent values — 13.5 (21.3%), 9.0 (19.1%), 11.0 (16.0%), 17.5 (15.4%), and 7.0 (11.5%) — together make up 83.4% of postings, and the first ten values 99.1%. In other words, the Iranian employer announces salary as a “band”, not a continuous value.

The composition of the numbers is also meaningful: 51.7% are integers and 48.3% end in “point five”. Figure 9.1-b shows that this pattern changes with the salary level — below 8 million, 68% are integers; in the 8 to 11 million range, 100% (that is, there is no half-unit number in this range); and above 15 million, only 18.8% (because 17.5 and 22.5 dominate). So the higher the salary, the more the employer uses half-million anchors.

Three methodological consequences: (1) the median and quartiles are fully meaningful, because they sit on these very anchors; (2) drawing a continuous “density” on this data is misleading — that is why in section 7 a histogram with bins was chosen, not KDE; (3) in the models we used log_salary so that the right skew is tamed and this discreteness does not have a damaging effect.

Interpretation of tables 9.9 and 9.10: Contrary to expectations, the picture of “small-sample groups” is empty in two dimensions: there is no province and no company size with fewer than 100 postings with salary — meaning the provincial analysis and the analysis that relies on company size stand on a sufficient sample.

The problem is in job category: 15 job categories out of the 73 categories with at least 20 postings (20.5%) have fewer than 100 postings with salary; the smallest are baker (23 postings, 11 with salary), butcher (35 and 16), and energy/nuclear engineering (69 and 19). At the industry level, only two industries are below the threshold: shipping (111 postings, 44 with salary) and translation (161 and 48).

The subtle point here is: the disclosure rate of these very small-sample groups (27% to 48%) is not lower than the market average (33.1%). So the small number is a product of the size of the category itself, not employer concealment. Practical implication: any salary median reported in sections 12 to 22 for these categories is a “signal”, not an “estimate”; in the interpretations we have respected this distinction, and in the comparison tables we have put the sample count next to each number.
